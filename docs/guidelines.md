# Set writing guidelines

Write the project's actual choices in `guidelines.json`. Use `voice`, `teaching`,
`content_boundaries`, `composition`, `word_budget` and `required_reviews`;
`extensions` can hold further project-specific rules. Prefix prose rules with
`Required:` or `Preference:` when their force could be unclear. The tool does not
automatically judge compliance with a prose rule.

Decide audience and reading situation first: shared read-aloud, independent
reading, screen viewing or a combination. Name the emotional promise, topics to
handle gently, language level and intended reader participation. Define review
needs before drafting, and adjust them when the format changes.

## Picture-book starting point: ages 3–6

These are editable craft targets, not a publishing specification. A 32-page book
often leaves about 12–14 story spreads after front and back matter; confirm the
actual page allocation. A 300–600-word read-aloud draft is a useful starting
range. Fully illustrated overlay editions can use substantially fewer words
provided the causes, feelings and resolution still make sense.

- Begin with a concrete want and a situation the child recognizes: sharing,
  waiting, a lost possession, getting ready or trying something new.
- Keep the central cast small. The protagonist makes the decisive choice;
  companions may support it. A character need not literally share the reader's
  age to have understandable wants and limited power.
- Build a causal arc: want, obstacle, attempts, discovery, changed action and
  satisfying result. Three attempts can create rhythm, but each must change
  knowledge, cost or strategy; add or remove attempts when the story needs it.
- Give mistakes visible consequences without humiliation. Let a feeling register
  before solving it. Show the body as well as naming the feeling simply.
- Let the ending demonstrate the theme. An implicit lesson is enough; a narrator
  does not need to explain the correct behavior. Warmth, effort and care matter
  more than obedience or prizes.
- Use concrete actions, mild surprise, playful sounds and child-scale humor.
  Avoid relying on sarcasm or adult irony. Obstacles may be misunderstandings,
  competing wants, weather or big feelings; a villain is seldom necessary.
- If teaching is part of the brief, choose one central concept or skill and let
  the hero use it. Repeat it meaningfully in varied situations. Three encounters
  are a useful target, not evidence that a child has learned it.
- Offer something to predict, count, spot, name or repeat when it fits. A reader
  question and a picture-supported stretch word can help without becoming
  compulsory interruptions.
- Let danger suit the agreed audience and resolve within the story. Keep the
  emotional stakes credible even when the physical stakes are small.
- Test every sentence aloud. Repetition needs a changing situation; short text
  still needs specific motives, actions and consequences.
- Give each spread one dominant action and a reason to turn the page. Let art
  add information rather than illustrate an already exhaustive description.
- End with a visible payoff, often a changed version of an opening action or
  refrain. Add visual details that reward rereading without confusing the plot.
